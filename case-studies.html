Who's Really Watching? Fixing Instagram's Story Replay Blind Spot

Summary

Instagram Pro users can't see who specifically rewatched their story — they only see a total replay count. On top of that, viewer data disappears after 48 hours, so even the limited data that exists doesn't stick around long enough to act on. I surveyed 8 people and confirmed both problems, though I also learned that half of respondents rarely or never check story insights at all.

I compared 4 possible fixes using RICE scoring. One idea scored slightly higher on paper, but I chose a different one — a new "Top Repeat Viewers" section with a longer 30-day data window — because it's backed by real complaints from the survey, not just a guess. The fix was broken into 10 tasks across two planned sprints.

Problem definition

What's broken: Instagram's Professional Dashboard shows aggregate story replay counts, but gives no breakdown of who rewatched a story or how many times each viewer replayed it. A story replayed 340 times by 340 different people looks identical in the data to one replayed 34 times by 10 highly engaged viewers — even though these represent very different levels of interest.

Who it affects: Creators and small businesses using Instagram Pro who rely on story insights to gauge audience interest and identify high-intent followers. Especially relevant for small businesses using Instagram as a sales channel, where a repeat viewer often signals a warmer lead.

Why it matters: Replay behaviour is one of the strongest passive engagement signals Instagram already collects — a user doesn't replay a story by accident. Surfacing who is repeatedly engaging would let businesses act on interest signals they're currently blind to.

Research findings

I surveyed 8 Instagram users to test the hypothesis. Key results:

  • 50% rarely or never check story insights; 12.5% didn't know the feature existed
  • 50% have wondered "who specifically" rewatched their story at least sometimes
  • 25% said knowing individual repeat viewers would definitely change how they use the app; another 37.5% said maybe

Two themes emerged from the open responses. The first confirmed the original hypothesis — "Cannot find who is rewatching the story multiple times" — though only one respondent raised it unprompted. The second was stronger and unprompted: "The list of people who viewed the stories vanish after 48 hours."

Decision: rather than treating these as separate problems, I scoped the fix to address both — adding per-viewer visibility and extending the retention window — since the research suggested they're connected symptoms of the same underlying need.

The weekly digest scored marginally highest, but its confidence was low — no respondent actually requested it. I chose the Top Repeat Viewers section instead: slightly lower score, but directly supported by two real complaints rather than speculation.

Execution plan

Dependencies: the backend retention changes need to land before the analytics events can be meaningfully tested — there's no point measuring usage of a feature with no real data behind it. Design work can run in parallel.

  • Week 1 — design the section layout, define ranking logic, extend retention policy, design the empty state
  • Week 2 — update backend storage for 30-day retention
  • Week 3 — add analytics tracking, write tooltip copy
  • Week 4 — QA, build the post-launch tracking dashboard, launch

Risks

  • Tracking individual viewers this closely might feel invasive → only show the data to the account owner, never notify the person being tracked
  • Longer retention costs more to store → start at 30 days, monitor cost, adjust
  • People might not notice the feature → ship the in-app tooltip alongside it, not after
  • Only 8 survey responses, so real behaviour could differ → track actual usage post-launch and be ready to change course
  • Backend retention work was probably estimated optimistically → flag it early as the riskiest task, check with engineering before locking the schedule
